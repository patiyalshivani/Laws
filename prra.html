Pre-Removal Risk Assessment (PRRA) guidance

A Pre-Removal Risk Assessment (PRRA) is a legal process in Canada that allows persons facing removal to apply for protection on the grounds they would face serious risk if returned to their country. This is often a final avenue before removal.

What a PRRA Is

A PRRA evaluates whether you would face:

  • Persecution.
  • Torture.
  • Risk to your life.
  • Cruel or unusual treatment or punishment.

The process is mostly written. A decision-maker reviews your submissions, evidence and updated country conditions. If approved, you may become a protected person, which can lead to permanent residence.

Who Can Apply

You can apply for PRRA only if:

  • You received a PRRA Notification Letter from the Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA).
  • You are under a removal order or scheduled for removal.
  • You meet timing requirements, including the filing deadline.
  • You are not blocked by a mandatory waiting period from a previous refusal.

You typically cannot apply if:

  • Your refugee claim or previous PRRA was refused less than 12 months ago, unless there is new evidence or significantly changed country conditions.
  • You are inadmissible for certain serious grounds that limit access to protection.
  • You do not meet the submission deadline.

A PRRA is time-sensitive. Missing deadlines can end your chance to apply.

What the Officer Reviews

  • You face personal, targeted risk, not generalized country hardship.
  • You cannot safely relocate to another part of your country.
  • Your government cannot or will not protect you.
  • The danger you face meets the legal threshold for protection.
  • You do not pose a danger to Canada’s security or public safety.

Certain factors, such as poverty, job loss or lack of health care, are not enough on their own unless tied to serious risk.

Possible Outcomes

1. Approved

You are recognized as a protected person. This stops your removal and allows you to apply for permanent residence.

2. Refused

Removal may proceed. You might still be able to file for judicial review in the Federal Court or pursue other remedies depending on your situation.

Sexual assault defence guidance

Sexual assault charges carry severe legal, social and personal consequences. These cases require careful analysis of evidence, credibility and the circumstances surrounding the alleged encounter. Understanding how sexual assault is defined and proven in Canada is essential to building a strong defence.

Proving Sexual Assault

Sexual assault is an assault where the touching is sexual in nature and violates the sexual integrity of the complainant.

To secure a conviction, the prosecution must prove three elements:

  • The accused intentionally touched the complainant.
  • The touching was sexual in nature.
  • The complainant did not consent.

If any of these elements is not proven beyond a reasonable doubt, the charge cannot be upheld.

What Counts as Assault, Sexual Assault and Consent

Assault

An assault occurs when someone intentionally applies force to another person without their consent. Even minor contact may qualify if it is intentional and non-consensual.

Sexual Assault

After proving an assault, the Crown must show the touching had a sexual nature or sexual purpose. Courts assess the body part touched, the type of contact, the circumstances surrounding the interaction, and the intent of the accused.

Sexual gratification is not required. A sexual assault can occur even without sexual motives if the touching involves sexual integrity.

Consent

Consent must be voluntary, informed and ongoing at the time the touching occurred. The judge evaluates consent from the complainant’s perspective at the moment of the encounter, not based on regret after the fact. Silence, passivity or ambiguous behaviour do not amount to consent.

Consent does not exist when:

  • Someone else gives consent on the complainant’s behalf.
  • The person lacks mental capacity due to intoxication, unconsciousness or illness.
  • Consent is obtained through authority, trust, manipulation or power imbalance.
  • The person expresses disagreement or stops participating.
  • The person is legally unable to consent due to age.

Age of Consent in Canada

The legal age to consent to sexual activity is 16, with close-in-age exceptions.

Common Defences in Sexual Assault Cases

Consent

If the evidence shows the complainant voluntarily agreed to the activity and did not withdraw consent, this is a complete defence.

Honest but Mistaken Belief in Consent

An accused may argue they honestly, but mistakenly, believed the complainant consented. There must be evidence showing words or actions that reasonably signalled agreement.

Identity

If the Crown cannot prove the identity of the perpetrator beyond a reasonable doubt, the charge cannot stand.

No Sexual Touching Occurred

A person may deny that the contact was sexual or dispute that any sexual contact took place.

Accident

Accidental touching, without intent, cannot form the basis of a sexual assault.

Limits on Challenging the Complainant’s Credibility

Courts impose strict limits on questioning about a complainant’s sexual history. A defence lawyer must seek court permission before introducing this type of evidence. A judge may allow it only if it is relevant to a real issue and has meaningful probative value.

Speaking to Police

Anyone accused of sexual assault should consult a criminal defence lawyer before speaking to police. Statements made to officers can be used against you, and charges are often laid based solely on the complainant’s account. Early legal guidance is essential.

Other Sexual Offence Charges

In addition to sexual assault, the Criminal Code includes related offences such as:

  • Sexual exploitation.
  • Sexual interference.
  • Invitation to sexual touching.
  • Voyeurism.
  • Child luring.
  • Indecent acts.

Sentencing and the Sex Offender Registry

Sexual assault sentences can range from probation to lengthy imprisonment depending on the offence level, harm, and circumstances. Many convictions result in mandatory inclusion on the national sex offender registry, which significantly impacts employment, travel and future personal freedom.
